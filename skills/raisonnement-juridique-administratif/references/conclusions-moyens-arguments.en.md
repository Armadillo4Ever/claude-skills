# Claims, pleas, arguments

French sources are quoted in free translation; the French text prevails.

## The distinction

**Claims (*conclusions*).** What is asked of the judge. They appear in the operative part of the written pleadings. Examples: the annulment of a decision, an order that the administration pay a sum, an injunction with or without a periodic penalty payment, the costs incurred and not included in the taxable costs.

The judge rules on all the claims, and only on them. Each head of claim calls for an article of the operative part or a ground of dismissal.

**Pleas (*moyens*).** The reasons, of law or of fact, invoked in support of the claims. The judge answers them in the grounds, subject to the economy of pleas.

**Arguments.** The elements of discussion with no legal effect of their own, which merely support a plea. The judge does not answer them. Answering them weighs down the decision and creates superfluous grounds that may be open to criticism.

Practical test: if the element, assuming it well founded, is sufficient to uphold a claim, it is a plea. Otherwise, it is an argument.

## The legal causes of action

The pleas in an action for annulment for excess of power (*recours pour excès de pouvoir*) attach to two distinct legal causes of action:

- **external legality**: lack of competence of the author of the act, procedural defect, defect of form;
- **internal legality**: breach of the rule of law, error of law, error of fact, error in legal characterisation, manifest error of assessment, misuse of power.

Procedural consequence: after the expiry of the time limit for bringing the action, the applicant may no longer invoke a plea falling under a legal cause of action he had not raised within that time limit. This is the rule stemming from the Intercopie case law. Pleas of public policy escape this limit.

Before applying this rule in a draft, find the decision in the corpus and cite its exact verbatim text. Do not cite it from memory.

## Order of examination

The usual order is as follows:

1. jurisdiction of the court;
2. admissibility of the application, including time limit, standing, prior crystallisation of the dispute (*liaison du contentieux*) where relevant;
3. existence of a dispute to be decided, possible no need to adjudicate;
4. external legality;
5. internal legality;
6. ancillary claims: injunction, periodic penalty payment, costs.

This order may be departed from, in particular when the judge chooses to uphold directly a plea of internal legality in order to give the annulment a useful scope. That choice is reasoned; it is not suffered.

## Drafting

Restate the pleas in the parties' own terms before answering them, without rewording them to the point of changing their scope. A rewording that shifts the plea exposes the decision to distortion.

Group together the pleas that call for a common answer, and say so.

Expressly set aside the pleas that are not answered because of the economy of pleas, rather than passing over them in silence.

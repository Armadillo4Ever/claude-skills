# Syllogism and statement of grounds

French sources are quoted in free translation; the French text prevails.

## Style

Since the reform of the drafting of decisions of the administrative courts, the grounds are written in direct style, in numbered paragraphs, without the "considérant que" formula ("whereas"). Sentences are short, in the present indicative, without unnecessary parentheses.

Reference: Council of State (*Conseil d'État*), Vade-mecum on the drafting of decisions of the administrative courts.
Link: https://www.conseil-etat.fr/content/download/152050/file/Vade-mecum-Redaction-decisions-de-la-juridiction-administrative.pdf

## Major premise

State the applicable rule.

- Cite the text in the version applicable to the dispute, and not in its current version. Check the dates of entry into force and the transitional provisions.
- Reproduce the useful text, not the whole article where only one sentence is at issue.
- Add, where appropriate, the interpretation adopted by the case law, citing the decision and its level of publication.
- Specify the conditions of application and the rules of evidence.

Usual wording: "Under the terms of article X of code Y: '...'. It follows from these provisions that ..." (*Aux termes de l'article X du code Y : "…". Il résulte de ces dispositions que …*).

Never state a major premise from memory. Retrieve the full text of the article and the verbatim text of the decision cited.

## Minor premise

Apply the rule to the facts.

- Start from what is established by the investigation, referring to the exhibits by their nature, date and author.
- Distinguish what is undisputed from what is contested.
- Answer the pleas (*moyens*), in the order adopted.
- Characterise the facts legally, an operation distinct from finding them.

Usual wording: "It is apparent from the documents in the file that ..." (*Il ressort des pièces du dossier que …*). For what falls within the sovereign assessment of the trial judges, the wording differs from what falls within the review of legal characterisation: check the wording appropriate to the review exercised.

## Conclusion

Draw the consequence of the minor premise, in strict consistency with the major premise, and carry it into the operative part. A statement of grounds that concludes that there is illegality must be echoed in an article of the operative part pronouncing the annulment.

## What has no place in the grounds

- A hypothetical ground: "even supposing that", used other than in an assumed subsidiary answer.
- A dubitative ground: "it seems", "probably".
- A superfluous ground, which supports no conclusion.
- A value judgment on a person.
- A consideration of expediency where the review is a review of legality.
- A reference to legal scholarship. Scholarship enlightens the rapporteur, it does not found the decision.

## Final check

Before closing, check point by point:

1. each head of claim has received an answer, in the operative part or by a ground of dismissal;
2. no ground supports a non-existent claim;
3. each text cited is cited in its applicable version, with its full text verified;
4. each decision cited is cited with its formation and its level of publication;
5. every plea raised of the court's own motion has been communicated to the parties;
6. the operative part is executable as drafted.

## Worked example

The formulas matter as much as the structure. The three specimens below can be read in a minute and fix the usage. They do not claim exclusivity: several ways of drafting coexist in practice to answer a plea, and what matters is that the structure of the syllogism be legible in them. The references to texts are deliberately left blank, in the form "article X of code Y": the point concerns form, and the rule forbidding the citation of a text without having retrieved its applicable version applies to an example too.

### A plea set aside

> 3. Under the terms of article X of code Y, in the version applicable to the dispute: "[useful text]". It follows from these provisions that the competent authority may refuse [the authorisation at issue] only where [conditions], and that it is for that authority to assess [subject of the review] at the date on which it takes its decision.
>
> 4. It is apparent from the documents in the file, in particular [nature of the exhibit] of [date] issued by [author], that [established fact]. While Mr A argues that [plea restated in its own terms], it is however apparent from the same documents that [contrary fact]. Consequently, the plea alleging that [characterisation of the plea] must be set aside.

Three things are at play here. The major premise states the rule and its conditions, not only the text. The minor premise refers to the exhibit by its nature, date and author, never by an approximate designation. And the plea is restated before being set aside, in the party's own terms, failing which the answer bears on something other than what was argued.

### A plea upheld, followed by the operative part

> 6. It follows from article Z of code Y that [rule of competence or of procedure]. Compliance with this requirement constitutes [a safeguard / a substantial formality].
>
> 7. It is apparent from the documents in the file that [finding of the breach]. The administration does not dispute [undisputed element] and does not establish that [circumstance that could have neutralised the defect]. Accordingly, Mr A is entitled to argue that the contested decision was taken at the end of an irregular procedure.
>
> 8. It follows from the foregoing, without there being any need to examine the other pleas of the application, that Mr A is entitled to seek the annulment of the decision of [date].
>
> **Decides:** (*Décide :*)
>
> **Article 1:** The decision of [authority] of [date] is annulled. (*Article 1er : La décision du [autorité] du [date] est annulée.*)

Paragraph 8 does two things in one sentence: it draws the consequence and it assumes the economy of pleas by stating it. The formula "without there being any need to examine the other pleas" (*sans qu'il soit besoin d'examiner les autres moyens*) is not a stylistic clause, it is what explains to the reader why the remaining pleas receive no answer. Omitting it turns an economy of pleas into an apparent failure to rule.

### A defective ground and its correction

Defective:

> It seems that the administration did not sufficiently state the reasons for its decision, which moreover appears hardly expedient in view of the situation of the person concerned.

Three defects in one sentence. "It seems" and "appears" are dubitative: the judge finds, he does not conjecture. "Moreover" introduces a superfluous ground. And expediency lies outside the review of legality.

Corrected:

> The contested decision merely refers to [the text] without stating the considerations of law and of fact that constitute its basis. It is thus insufficiently reasoned.
